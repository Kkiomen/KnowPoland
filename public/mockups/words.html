Six words · about fifteen minutes

You don't need
the language.
You need six words.

Polish looks unpronounceable and mostly isn't - the spelling is consistent, so once you know the sounds you can read any sign aloud. These six will change how you are treated, and each one explains something about the country that no guidebook paragraph does.

Word 01 · the one you use most

Dzień dobry

jen DOH-bri

Good day. The greeting for every stranger, every time.

What it tells you

You say it entering a shop, a lift, a waiting room, a compartment on a train - to people you will never speak to again. Walking in silently is not neutral here; it reads as rude. Two syllables buy you an entirely different reception.

The greetings entry →
Careful

After roughly 6 p.m. it becomes dobry wieczór - good evening. Nobody minds if you get it wrong.

Word 02 · the one that opens doors

Dziękuję

jen-KOO-yeh

Thank you - and, said at the right moment, “no thanks, that's enough”.

What it tells you

Poles are less effusive than Americans and less formal than Germans, but thanks is expected and returned. Said while covering your glass, it also means “stop pouring” - which is a useful thing to know at a table in the countryside.

Word 03 · the one nobody teaches you

Bar mleczny

bar MLETCH-ni

“Milk bar” - a subsidised canteen serving home cooking at very low prices.

What it tells you

They were built to feed factory workers, survived the end of communism, and are still subsidised by the state today. Queue, order at the counter, carry your own tray, share a table. A full lunch costs less than a coffee in most European capitals.

Word 04 · the one that saves you money

Kantor

KAN-tor

An exchange office. There is one on nearly every central street.

What it tells you

Poland is in the European Union but not in the euro - you will be paying in złoty. Rates in a town-centre kantor are normally better than at the airport or in a hotel, and cards work almost everywhere anyway, including on buses and at market stalls.

Word 05 · the one that signals respect

Pan / Pani

pan / PAH-nee

Sir / madam - but used as the ordinary word for “you” with anyone you don't know.

What it tells you

Polish keeps a hard line between formal and informal address, and crossing it early is the fastest way to seem rude. Use pan or pani with waiters, drivers, officials and anyone older, until they suggest otherwise. English flattens this distinction; Polish does not.

Word 06 · the one you'll hear raised

Na zdrowie

na ZDROH-vyeh

To your health - a toast, and also what you say when somebody sneezes.

What it tells you

Toasts are made looking the other person in the eye, and hospitality is insistent: refusing food twice is normal, and offering it three times is also normal. If you are invited to someone's home, bring flowers in an odd number - even numbers are for funerals.
