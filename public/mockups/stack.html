Poland changes completely as you go south: dunes, then two thousand lakes, then flat farmland, then a limestone plateau, then granite. Take the descent one landscape at a time - the gauge in the corner keeps track of where you are.

Begin at sea level →
STAGE 0154.6°N · Baltic coast · sea level

The cold sea

Five hundred kilometres of coastline, most of it sand. The water is brackish and rarely warm, the dunes at Łeba move several metres a year, and the Hel peninsula is a thirty-kilometre spit of sand you can walk the width of in ten minutes. Amber washes up after storms; it has been traded from here since antiquity.

What it explains

Access to this coast - Gdańsk, Szczecin, the ports - is what half of Poland's wars were about.

STAGE 0253.8°N · Masuria & Podlasie · 120 m

Two thousand lakes

The glaciers left the north-east covered in water - a landscape of interconnected lakes you can sail across for days. Further east stands Białowieża, one of the last fragments of the primeval forest that once covered the continent, and the home of the European bison, brought back from near-extinction after the First World War.

What it explains

Why the east feels emptier, older and more Orthodox than the rest of the country.

STAGE 0352.2°N · the central plain · 100 m

The flat middle

Most of Poland is a plain - which is the single most important fact in its history. There is nothing here to stop an army, in either direction, and there never was. Warsaw sits in the middle of it, on a river that runs the whole length of the country, from the mountains to the sea.

STAGE 0450.1°N · the limestone uplands · 220 m

Rock, salt
and old towns

South of the plain the ground rises into limestone: caves, ruined castles on white crags, and a salt mine worked continuously from the 13th century until 1996. Kraków sits at the edge of it - the one big city the Second World War left standing, which is why it looks the way the others were rebuilt to look.

What it explains

Why the south is the oldest-looking part of the country, and the most visited.

STAGE 0549.2°N · the Tatras · 2 499 m

Granite, finally

The Tatras are small - fifty kilometres end to end - and genuinely alpine: bare granite, chamois, marmots and weather that turns in twenty minutes. Rysy, at 2 499 m, is the highest point in the country. Below sits Zakopane, with its own wooden architecture, its own dialect and a highlander culture older than the modern state.
