FRAME 01

966

A duke is baptised

Mieszko I accepts Christianity and pulls a cluster of Slavic tribes into Latin Europe - western alphabet, western law, a border argument that has not finished yet. Everything after this frame is a consequence of it.

Stand in it

Gniezno Cathedral, with bronze doors cast in the 1170s showing the country's first saint.

FRAME 02

1791

A constitution, four years too late

The Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth - elected kings, a parliament any single deputy could paralyse - writes Europe's first modern codified constitution on 3 May. Its neighbours respond by finishing the partition of the country in 1795.

Stand in it

The Royal Castle in Warsaw, where it was adopted - and which was itself rebuilt after 1971.

FRAME 03

1795

Off the map for 123 years

Russia, Prussia and Austria divide what is left. Four generations grow up without a state - but with a language, a literature and a habit of uprising. It is why 1918 restores a country rather than inventing one.

Stand in it

Any 19th-century city centre: three empires built them, and you can still tell which is which.

Read the chapter

FRAME 04

1939

The war starts here

Germany invades on 1 September, the Soviet Union on the 17th. The occupation kills millions of Polish citizens, including almost the entire pre-war Jewish population, and ends with Warsaw deliberately levelled. This frame is not scenery; it is the reason for most of what you'll see.

Visit it properly

Westerplatte, the Warsaw Rising Museum, Auschwitz-Birkenau - booked ahead, entered quietly.

Read the chapter

FRAME 05

1980

A strike that outlasted a system

Workers occupy the Gdańsk shipyard and win an independent trade union - ten million members within a year. Martial law follows in 1981; the June 1989 elections finish the argument, and the rest of the bloc goes within months.

Stand in it

The European Solidarity Centre, built against gate number two of the shipyard.

FRAME 06

Now

The country you'll actually land in

Mountains in the south, two thousand lakes in the north-east, a Baltic coast, and very flat in between. Card payments everywhere, trains that run on time, the złoty rather than the euro - and a milk bar on the corner serving the same lunch it served in 1975.

Money, trains, SIM cards, tipping and memorial-site etiquette - each note dated.
